The Gym Rule: Using AI Tools Without Letting Them Do Your Thinking

Breaking Out November 24, 2026

Nobody hires a forklift to do their deadlifts. The forklift is great at lifting. That is exactly why it is useless for building the muscle.

That is the cleanest way I know to think about how to use AI tools without letting them do my thinking. Some tasks are about getting the weight from point A to point B. Some tasks are about getting stronger. The trouble starts when you use the forklift on the second kind and then wonder why you feel weak.

First, Sort Your Work Into Moving and Building

Before you open a chat window, ask one question: Is this task about the output, or about me?

Moving tasks are about the output. Reformatting a spreadsheet, summarizing a long document you only need the gist of, drafting a polite decline email, converting notes to a clean list. Nobody grows from these. Let the forklift run.

Building tasks are about you. Forming an opinion, deciding what your project is actually about, finding the angle, choosing between two options when the tradeoffs are real. These are the reps. If a tool does them, the product might look fine, but the muscle you walked in with is the muscle you walk out with.

Most people never make this sort, so everything gets the forklift. That is where the slow thinning of your own thinking comes from.

The Warning Signs You Have Over-Delegated

You do not need a dramatic realization. The signs are small and a little embarrassing:

  • You open the tool before you have had a single thought of your own about the problem.
  • You cannot explain, out loud and without notes, why you chose the thing you chose.
  • Your drafts all share the same smooth, agreeable rhythm, and you cannot tell which parts were yours.
  • A blank page feels more uncomfortable than it used to.
  • You accept the first answer more often than you used to, because it is good enough and you are tired.

If two or three of those landed, welcome. That is the normal result of a powerful tool sitting one click away. It does not mean you are lazy. It means the friction that used to force you to think has been removed, and you have to put some back on purpose.

Put the Friction Back: Four Habits That Work

1. Commit before you consult

Write down your answer first. One sentence is enough. "I think we should lead with the price, because that is what people ask about first." Now you have a position. When the AI responds, you are comparing, not absorbing. Without a stake in the ground, you will drift toward whatever you read last.

2. Use it as the second voice, never the first

Ask the tool to poke holes, not to produce the plan. "Here is my plan. What am I missing? What would a skeptical customer say?" The thinking stays yours, and the tool earns its keep by stress-testing it. The difference in what you learn is huge.

3. Make it show the fork, not the path

Instead of "what should I do," try "give me three genuinely different approaches and the tradeoff of each." Then you choose. The choosing is the part worth keeping. A tool that hands you one confident path trains you to follow. A tool that hands you a fork trains you to decide.

4. Do a no-tool rep on purpose

Once a week, take a real problem and work it start to finish with nothing but a notebook. It will feel slow. That slowness is the point. You are keeping the capacity alive so that when you do use the tool, you are directing it instead of leaning on it.

A Quick Test for Any Single Prompt

Right before you hit enter, ask yourself:

  1. Could I explain this answer to someone else without looking at it? If the tool's answer would go straight from the screen to your project with nothing passing through your head, you are skipping a rep.
  2. Do I have an opinion already? If yes, write it down first. If no, spend five minutes forming one before you ask.
  3. Is this a moving task or a building task? Be honest. Moving, go ahead. Building, slow down.

That is thirty seconds of overhead, and it prevents most of the damage.

What About When You Genuinely Have Nothing?

Sometimes your head is empty. Real thing. Tired, stuck, out of angles. Using the tool to get unstuck is fine, as long as you treat what comes back as raw material, not as a verdict.

Ask for a pile instead of a solution: ten rough directions, a list of questions you have not considered, a few ways other people have framed this problem. Then react. Which ones annoy you? Which one makes you say "no, that is wrong, it is actually more like this"? That reaction, the pushback, is your own thinking switching back on. Use the tool to wake it up, not to replace it.

Keep Your Taste, Because It Is the Whole Game

Here is the part that matters long term. As these tools get better, the output gets easier for everyone to produce. What stays scarce is judgment: knowing which of the ten good options is the right one for your situation, your audience, your voice. Taste is built by making calls and living with them. Every time you outsource the call, you skip a deposit into that account.

You do not need to swear off the tools. You need to be deliberate about which weight you hand to the forklift and which you pick up yourself. If you want a tighter, more specific version of this idea, the companion piece, Keeping the Thought That Belongs to You: A Working Rule for AI-Assisted Thinking, boils it down to a single rule you can carry into any session.

The Short Version

Sort the work. Commit to a view before you consult. Use the tool to challenge you, not to replace you. Take a few reps with no assist. Keep the calls that make you better.

The goal was never to avoid powerful tools. It is to stay someone who would still have something worth saying if the tab were closed. Do the lifting that makes you strong, and let the forklift handle the freight.
