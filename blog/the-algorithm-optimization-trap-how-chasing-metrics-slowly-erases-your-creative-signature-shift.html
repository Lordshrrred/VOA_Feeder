The Slow Sanding: What Optimizing for the Feed Actually Does to Your Work

Breaking Out October 29, 2026

Nobody wakes up and decides to make boring work. That's the thing. You make one post that lands, you notice the numbers, and a tiny voice says "do that again." Fair enough. Why wouldn't you?

But I want to talk about what's actually happening underneath, because the answer to "what happens to your creative work when you optimize for the algorithm instead of your taste" isn't a dramatic collapse. It's closer to sanding. Every pass takes off a little roughness, and roughness turns out to be where the grain was.

The Sanding Metaphor, Taken Seriously

Picture a piece of wood with a strange knot in it. Sand it enough and it becomes smooth, even, and indistinguishable from every other board in the lumber yard. Nobody complains about the smoothness. They just stop remembering which board was yours.

Platforms work the same way. They surface what has already performed, so the safest route to reach is a route that's already crowded. Follow it long enough and you converge on the average of everything that came before you. Your work doesn't get worse by any measure you can chart. It gets interchangeable, which is a different and sneakier problem.

What Goes First

The losses follow a pretty predictable order. The first things to go are the ones that cost you nothing on a dashboard but cost you everything in character:

  • The odd opening. The slow start, the weird sentence, the image with no explanation. These hurt early retention, so they get replaced by a hook format you saw working elsewhere.
  • The long pause. Silence, breathing room, a beat that lets something sink in. Gone, because dead air looks like a drop-off.
  • The inside reference. The thing only your people would catch. It doesn't travel, so it gets cut.
  • The strange true title. Swapped for something searchable and vague.
  • The opinion. "I think" becomes "some people say." Commitment is risky. Hedging is free.

Notice that every one of those is a place where you made a specific choice. Specific choices are friction. Friction is what makes a voice recognizable. The algorithm doesn't punish voice on purpose, it just can't see it, and what it can't see you stop doing.

The Archive Problem Nobody Mentions

Here's a quieter effect. When you stop posting the pieces that underperform, your public body of work starts to lie about you. It shows a version of you who only ever made winners, which means the experiments, the failures that taught you something, and the weird side roads all disappear.

That matters because your archive is also how you remember yourself. Flip back through it a year later and you'll see a very consistent, very competent stranger. Competent is not the compliment it sounds like.

Your Taste Doesn't Die, It Atrophies

The scariest version of this isn't that the feed overrules your taste. It's that you stop using it. Taste is a muscle. You exercise it by making choices you can't justify with data, and when every choice gets routed through "will this perform?", the muscle goes slack.

Then one day you sit down to make something and you honestly can't tell what you want. Not because you have no preferences, but because you can't separate yours from the ones the feed has been feeding you. That fog is the real cost, and it's why this is worth taking seriously even if your numbers look fine.

Why Performing Work Can Still Feel Hollow

You can hit your numbers and still feel nothing about the thing you made. That mismatch confuses people, so let me name it plainly: a metric measures attention, and attention is not the same as connection.

Optimized work gets seen by a lot of people who feel mildly fine about it. Honest, specific work gets seen by fewer people, some of whom feel something sharp. The first is a bigger number. The second is the reason anyone follows a person instead of a format.

A smaller audience that actually met you beats a larger one that scrolled past a very polished stranger.

A Field Test for Getting Your Signal Back

Don't try to think your way out of this. Your instincts are the contaminated instrument, so use evidence and constraints instead.

Step 1: Run the "only me" audit

Pull your last ten pieces. Next to each one, write a single choice that only you would have made: a subject, a texture, a pace, a thing you refused to include. If you can't name one, mark it. Those are the pieces the feed helped make.

Step 2: Go back before the numbers

Find work from before you watched stats closely, if it exists. Ask what you were allowed to be then that you aren't now. You're not trying to return to it, just to remember the range.

Step 3: Make one thing you won't measure

Create one small piece this week and don't check the numbers for seven days. Pay attention to your body when you resist. Relief and itchy dread are both useful data. This is an experiment, not a diagnosis.

Step 4: Decide the rule before you post

Set your question in advance, while you're calm: "Did I make what I meant to make?" Answer it before you look at any graph. That ordering is the whole trick, because once you see the number it colors the verdict.

Where Metrics Actually Belong

I'm not telling you to burn the dashboard. Numbers are a rearview mirror. They tell you what happened after people saw your work, which is genuinely useful information.

The mistake is using them as a steering wheel. Look at the mirror to ask a better question than "how do I get more of this?" Ask "who responded, and were they my people?" If the right ten people lit up, that's a win the chart will never celebrate.

The Honest Dodge Underneath It All

One more uncomfortable thing. Optimizing can be a very respectable way of avoiding the risky piece. The metric hands you a reason not to make the strange thing, and it wears a productivity costume so nobody questions it, including you.

If you catch yourself reaching for "what will perform" at the exact moment a real idea gets scary, that's not strategy. That's a flinch. Notice it kindly, then make the scary thing small enough to do anyway.

The Takeaway

Optimizing for the algorithm doesn't kill your creativity in one blow. It sands it, one reasonable edit at a time, until the grain you started with is hard to find. The fix isn't rebellion or ignoring data. It's keeping your taste in daily use so it stays yours, and letting the numbers inform you rather than instruct you.

Make something this week purely because you want to see it exist. Then notice how that feels. That feeling is the thing you're protecting.
