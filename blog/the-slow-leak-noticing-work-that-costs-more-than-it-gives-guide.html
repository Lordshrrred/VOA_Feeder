The Tank Test: 7 Ways to Tell If Your Job Is Quietly Draining You

Breaking Out October 30, 2026

Burnout gets all the headlines because it looks dramatic. Collapse, resignation letters, someone crying in a parking garage. But most people never hit a wall. They just get a little duller every quarter, and nobody notices, least of all them.

If you're trying to work out how to know if your job is slowly draining you, the hard part is that every individual symptom has an innocent explanation. You're tired because of sleep. You're irritable because of the news. You're flat because it's February. All true, maybe. But explanations stack up, and at some point the stack is the story.

So instead of asking "do I hate my job?" (a muddy question), try asking questions that have answers. Here are seven checks you can run this week.

Check 1: The Sunday Night Weather Report

Notice what happens in your body around 4pm on Sunday. Not your thoughts, your body. Tight chest, jaw clench, sudden urge to scroll or snack or start a fight about the dishes?

Everybody gets a little Sunday gloom. What matters is the trend. Is it the same as a year ago, or has it crept earlier, from Sunday night to Sunday noon to Saturday afternoon? A weather pattern that moves in one direction is a climate, not a bad day.

Check 2: The Recovery Ratio

Here's a measurement almost nobody takes. How long does it take you to feel like yourself after work ends?

  • Under an hour: Normal. Work took some energy, you got it back.
  • Most of the evening: Yellow flag. The job is eating into your actual life.
  • The whole weekend, and then it's Sunday again: Red flag. You're not recovering, you're just pausing.

A job that gives and takes in fair proportion lets you bounce back. A draining one makes recovery the entire point of your free time. If your weekends exist purely to get you functional for Monday, that's a job spending your life, not just your hours.

Check 3: What Happens When Good News Arrives

Run this one the next time something good happens at work. A compliment, a win, a project landing well. What do you feel?

If the honest answer is "relief that it's over" rather than "that felt good," pay attention. Draining work flattens the highs. You stop celebrating and start surviving, and a win just means one less threat on the board.

Check 4: The Stranger Test

Imagine a stranger reading a transcript of how you talk about your job for a week. Texts to friends, rants to your partner, the little sighs. Would they conclude you're building something or serving time?

Listen especially for the defensive stuff, the rehearsed lines like "it's stable" or "could be worse." Those aren't descriptions. They're legal arguments. Nobody drafts a closing statement for something that simply feels good.

Check 5: The Small-Self Inventory

Draining jobs rarely take big things. They take small ones, quietly:

  1. The side project you stopped mentioning
  2. The jokes you used to make
  3. The questions you used to ask in meetings
  4. The version of you who had opinions about the work

Write down three things you used to do or care about that have faded in the last two years. Then ask whether work is the common thread. Sometimes it isn't, and that's useful to know too. But if all three trace back to the same fifty hours a week, you've found your leak.

Check 6: Cost Versus Pay, in Honest Currency

We compare jobs by salary because it's the one number we can see. But a paycheck is only one side of the ledger. The other side is paid in things like sleep, patience with people you love, and how much you like yourself by Friday.

Try this: list what the job gives (money, stability, skills, people you like, flexibility) and what it costs (energy, health, mood, time, confidence). Be brutal and specific. Then ask which column you'd defend to a friend. Some jobs are worth a high cost, and that's a legitimate choice if you're making it with open eyes. The problem is never the cost itself. It's paying it without noticing.

Check 7: The Direction of the Slope

This is the big one, and it's the one most people skip. Don't ask "how do I feel now?" Ask "which way am I moving?"

A tough job on an upward slope (you're learning, you're growing, the hard part has an end) feels different from a comfortable job on a downward slope (you're coasting, shrinking, repeating year one for the fifth time). Two people can feel equally tired at work, and one is building while the other is leaking.

If you want a deeper walkthrough of this idea, including a way to track your energy over two weeks, the original piece on this lives at The Slow Leak: Noticing Work That Costs More Than It Gives. It's a good companion to the checks above.

A Quick Honesty Note

None of this is a diagnosis. Persistent exhaustion, loss of interest in everything, trouble sleeping, or feeling hopeless are things to bring to a doctor or qualified professional, because they can have causes that have nothing to do with your job. This article helps you read a pattern. It doesn't replace someone who can actually evaluate you.

What to Do With the Results

Say you ran the checks and the pattern is real. You don't have to quit on Monday. Quitting is one move, and often not the first one. Here's a saner order of operations:

Name the specific leak

"My job drains me" is too big to fix. "The Thursday reporting grind and the commute are draining me" is workable. Narrow it until you can point at it.

Test a small change

Can you change the thing, hand it off, batch it, renegotiate it, or protect the hour after it? One small experiment gives you real information about whether the job is the problem or just one corner of it.

Start a quiet exit file

Not a resignation, a file. Skills you want to build, people you'd like coffee with, roles that made you curious. Optionality is calming. Many people feel the drain ease just by knowing they have a plan B in a drawer.

Set a review date

Pick a date three months out and write it down: "If nothing has changed by then, I decide something." This turns vague suffering into a deadline, and a deadline is the cure for drift.

The Point

You don't need a crisis to be allowed to take your own energy seriously. Nothing has to be on fire. A tank that reads a little lower each month deserves a look, and looking costs you nothing but an honest hour. Run the checks, trust what repeats, and make the next move on purpose rather than by default.
