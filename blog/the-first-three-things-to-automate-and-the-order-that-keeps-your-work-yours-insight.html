Before You Automate Anything, Run the Wince Test

Breaking Out November 22, 2026

Every creative I know who got burned by automation made the same mistake. They automated the loudest thing first. The thing that was eating their attention, the thing that felt like a bottleneck, the thing a fan could see. It felt like momentum. It was actually a slow leak.

If you are asking what should I automate in my creative business first, here is a blunt little filter I use before I let a tool near anything. I call it the wince test.

The Wince Test, Explained

Picture a fan you actually like. Someone who has bought your work, shown up to your stuff, maybe sent you a kind note once. Now imagine they lean over and ask, "Hey, how does that part of your business work?"

Then you have to answer honestly, out loud, with their eyes on you.

  • If you would happily explain it ("oh, that just backs up my files every night"), it passes.
  • If you would shuffle a little and say "well, it kind of sends something that looks personal," you winced. It fails.

That flinch is data. It is your gut telling you the thing you built is performing a closeness that does not exist. Anything that makes you wince stays manual, or stays unbuilt, until you can answer without the wince.

Why Order Matters More Than the Tools

People obsess over which app to pick. The app matters about ten percent. The sequence matters about ninety. Automate in the wrong order and you spend months repairing trust. Automate in the right order and the same tools quietly give you hours back.

The principle underneath it: move boring information before you move anything that sounds like you. Machines are great at carrying things from one place to another. They are terrible at being you, and fans can feel the difference even when they cannot name it.

The Green Zone: Things Nobody Will Ever Read

Start where there is no audience at all. This is the safest ground you have, and it is where most solo creators should spend their first month.

Your private record-keeping

Release dates. Who you owe an invoice. A running dump of song, scene, or product ideas. A log of what you posted and when so you stop guessing what performed. A simple form that drops entries into a spreadsheet, or a recurring calendar nudge, covers all of it.

This passes the wince test instantly because there is nothing to explain. And if it breaks, the cost is a gap in your own notes, not a confused customer.

The tripwire to watch

The danger here is not failure, it is neglect. A tracker you stop opening becomes a graveyard of stale numbers, and you start making decisions off data you no longer trust. If you have not looked at your own dashboard in a month, either delete it or schedule a weekly five-minute look. A dead tracker is worse than no tracker.

The Yellow Zone: Plumbing That Carries Facts

Once your private system holds, move to the invisible machinery of delivering things. Not words that express you, just stuff that has to get from point A to point B.

  • Nightly backups of your project folders
  • Consistent file naming on exports so you stop hunting for "final_v3_REAL"
  • A weekly summary email you send to yourself
  • Receipts dropping into one folder
  • Purchase confirmations and download links that only state facts

A download link has no personality to fake. A receipt does not need your voice. If one goes wrong, you rename a file or resend an email.

The tripwire to watch

The moment a tool starts making judgment calls you never defined, you have crossed a line. If software is deciding which version is "the final one" or which customers deserve "VIP" treatment, you did not automate logistics. You handed off taste. Pull it back.

The Orange Zone: The Clock, Not the Content

Only after the first two feel boring and reliable should you touch anything the public sees. And even then, you automate when, never what.

Sit down once, write or approve your posts in a single batch, then let a scheduler handle the timing. You are hiring a courier, not a ghostwriter. The content was still your decision, and you can pull any post before it goes live.

The tripwire to watch

Approval turning into reflex. If you are clicking "approve" without reading, the system has quietly stopped being yours. A good sign you have overdone it: you cannot remember what is sitting in your queue. Shorten the queue until you can recite it from memory.

The Red Zone: Do Not Automate (Yet, Maybe Ever)

Replies. Direct messages. Anything where a person reasonably believes another person is on the other end. This is where the wince test earns its keep, because "personalized" auto-messages are the classic thing creators regret.

People are more perceptive than the software assumes. A fake-warm message does not read as warm, it reads as a stranger wearing your jacket. One cold exchange can undo a lot of earned goodwill, and you cannot unsend it.

If the whole point of the thing is that you showed up, then you have to show up.

A Simple Way to Sequence Your Own Week

  1. List everything repetitive. Do not filter yet. Just dump it.
  2. Run the wince test on each item. Cross off anything that makes you flinch.
  3. Sort the survivors by visibility. Private first, public last.
  4. Pick one. Not three. One. Build it, use it for two weeks, then decide if it earned a spot.
  5. Review monthly. Ask what you stopped looking at and what you stopped reading.

That fourth step is where most people rush. Automating five things at once means you cannot tell which one is quietly breaking. One at a time keeps the cause and effect visible.

What You Are Actually Protecting

Automation is not the enemy of a creative practice. Drudgery is. The goal is to claw back the hours that admin steals so those hours go to the actual making. But the trade only works if the work stays recognizably yours. The minute a fan senses a machine pretending to be you, you have spent trust to save minutes, and that is a terrible exchange rate.

The Short Version

Automate the boring stuff nobody sees. Then the plumbing that moves facts. Then, carefully, the timing of things you already approved. Leave the conversations alone.

And before you build anything, picture that fan leaning over and asking how it works. If you wince, you already know the answer.
