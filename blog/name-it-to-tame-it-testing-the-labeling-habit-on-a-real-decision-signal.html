The 90-Second Word Trick: Giving Your Feelings a Name Before They Sign Your Name

Breaking Out November 7, 2026

Here is a thing nobody warns you about: your emotions are very good at forging your signature.

You didn't decide to snap at your coworker, quit the side project, or text your ex. A feeling decided, then handed you the paperwork and let you believe it was your idea. The trick to taking your signature back is not feeling less. It's catching the forger in the act.

If you want how to name emotions so they stop controlling my decisions in its simplest form, it's this: a feeling with a name is a feeling you can interview. A feeling without one is a feeling that drives.

Why Unnamed Feelings Run the Show

An unnamed emotion doesn't announce itself as an emotion. It disguises itself as a fact. Resentment shows up as "honestly, this team doesn't deserve my effort." Fear shows up as "this just isn't a good time." Envy shows up as "that kind of success feels kind of fake, anyway."

Notice what happened there. The feeling skipped the line and got promoted straight to opinion. You're no longer experiencing resentment, you're simply being realistic. That disguise is the whole problem.

Naming rips off the costume. "I'm feeling resentful" and "this team is unworthy" are two completely different sentences, and only one of them is something you can check.

The Vocabulary Problem (Yes, It's a Real Problem)

Most of us run on about four emotion words: good, bad, stressed, fine. That's like trying to cook with only salt. Researchers who study emotional granularity, including Lisa Feldman Barrett's work on how people construct emotional experience, suggest that people who can distinguish between finer-grained feelings tend to handle them with more flexibility. Take it as a promising direction, not a guarantee, but it matches what most people find in practice: sharper words, sharper choices.

Here's a quick upgrade chart. Find your lazy word, then pick the real one hiding under it.

If you said "stressed," it might be:

  • Dread, because something specific and unpleasant is coming
  • Overwhelm, because too many things are arriving with no sorting system
  • Pressure, because someone else's expectations are sitting on your chest
  • Guilt, because you're behind on something that matters to someone

If you said "angry," it might be:

  • Disrespected, because a line got crossed
  • Powerless, because you can't change the thing that's bugging you
  • Embarrassed, because anger is a much cooler outfit than shame
  • Disappointed, because you wanted something and it didn't come

If you said "off" or "meh," it might be:

  • Bored, because nothing here is asking enough of you
  • Lonely, because you haven't been properly seen in a while
  • Tired, the physical kind, which is not a character flaw
  • Numb, because feeling it all got too loud

You don't need a perfect word. You need a better word than the one you had. Closer is the goal, not exact.

The 90-Second Routine

Do this when you notice a decision forming and your body has opinions about it. A tight chest, hot face, clenched jaw, or that urgent "I have to do this right now" feeling are all good flags. Urgency, by the way, is itself a clue. Most real decisions can survive a short pause.

  1. Stop and locate it. Where is it in your body? Throat, stomach, shoulders? Putting a location on it makes it a thing instead of a fog.
  2. Try two or three words. Say them in your head or write them down. "Angry? No. Hurt? Closer. Embarrassed? Yeah, that one."
  3. Give it a rough size. A quick 1 to 10. Some feelings are loud but small, and some are quiet and enormous. Knowing which one you're dealing with changes how much weight to give it.
  4. Ask what it wants you to do. Finish the sentence: "This feeling wants me to ____." Send the email, hide, say yes, burn it down.
  5. Ask what you want to do. This is a separate question, and it's the whole point. Now there are two voices in the room instead of one.

That's it. No candles required, though I won't judge you.

What This Actually Changes (And What It Doesn't)

Let's stay honest here. Naming a feeling doesn't make it vanish, and it won't make a hard choice easy. If you're grieving, naming the grief doesn't end it. What it does is stop the grief from quietly making your calendar decisions for the next six weeks.

There's research suggesting that putting feelings into words is associated with a dampening of reactivity in emotion-related brain regions, a line of work often linked to the phrase "affect labeling." It's an active area, the findings aren't uniform, and this is a self-awareness habit, not a medical treatment. If your feelings are persistently overwhelming or tied to something heavy, talking to a qualified professional is the right move, full stop.

For ordinary everyday forgery, though, the habit earns its keep. The feeling still shows up. It just doesn't get to sign.

Three Places This Pays Off Fast

Money decisions

The purchase that feels like "I deserve this" is often loneliness or boredom wearing a nice jacket. Name it before you check out. Sometimes you still buy the thing. Now it's a choice.

Conflict

The reply you draft when you're "annoyed" reads very differently once you realize you're actually hurt. Hurt writes a different message than annoyed does, and usually a better one.

Creative work

Quitting a project at the messy middle is almost always fear of it being mediocre, not actual evidence that it's mediocre. Name the fear and you can keep working with it in the room. Common Ways This Goes Sideways

  • Using it as a weapon. "I'm just naming my feelings" is not a license to dump them on someone. The word is for you first.
  • Stopping at the label. Naming is step one, not the finish line. The question is always what you do next.
  • Demanding the perfect word. If you spend ten minutes hunting the exact term, you've turned a two-minute tool into a procrastination hobby.
  • Expecting it to feel good. Sometimes the right word lands like a small punch. That's usually a sign you found it.

A Tiny Experiment for This Week

Pick one decision you'll face in the next few days, something real but low-stakes. Before you act on it, run the routine. Write down the word, the number, and the sentence "this feeling wants me to ____." Then make the call and note whether it differs from what you'd have done on autopilot.

You might find it changes nothing. That's useful data too. Or you might find, like plenty of people do, that about a third of your "decisions" were actually feelings in a trench coat. Either way, you'll know whose signature is on the page.

You don't have to feel less. You just have to know who's talking.

Start with one word today. Make it a specific one.
